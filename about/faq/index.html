Frequently Asked Questions

Answers to the questions we are asked most often about the tools, the data behind them, and their limitations. If your question is not here, the manual goes into more depth, and the feedback form reaches us directly.

Who the tools are for

Who is the intended audience: the public, policymakers, or researchers?

All three, and the tools are arranged so that each can stop at the level of detail they need. The audience we design for first is councillors and local authority officers developing decarbonisation policy, because they hold many of the levers and are rarely given evidence at neighbourhood scale. Community groups use the tools to make a local case, and researchers use the underlying data directly. Every chart carries a plain-language Overview, a Policy note on why the measure matters for decision making, and a Methods note on where the numbers come from.

What can a local authority actually do with this?

The most common uses we see are targeting: identifying which neighbourhoods should be first in line for an area-based retrofit scheme, evidencing a bus service improvement plan with twenty years of frequency data, and testing whether a proposed development site is somewhere people could live without a car. The tools are evidence for those decisions, not a substitute for local knowledge or survey work. We would rather you treated a striking result as a question to investigate than as a finding to act on directly.

Can I use screenshots and figures in a report or a council paper?

Yes. Please cite Morgan (2026) and link to www.carbon.place. Note that some basemaps carry third-party copyright, so check the manual before reproducing a screenshot that uses the satellite or historical basemaps. The data page sets out the licence conditions in full.

Funding and governance

Who funded this work?

Carbon & Place is funded by UK Research and Innovation, through the Engineering and Physical Sciences Research Council and the Economic and Social Research Council, via the Energy Demand Research Centre (grant EP/Y010078/1). It forms part of the EDRC Place theme. The original Place-Based Carbon Calculator was funded through the Centre for Research into Energy Demand Solutions (grant EP/R035288/1), which closed in 2023.

Will the data be updated?

Yes, though not on a fixed schedule. Each dataset is rebuilt when its source publishes an update, so the most recent year differs between charts and is stated on each one. Funding for the current phase runs to 2028. We cannot promise indefinite maintenance, which is one reason the analysis code and most of the outputs are published openly: if this project ends, the work can be continued by others.

About the data

Where do the emissions figures for each neighbourhood come from?

From three kinds of source, and the difference between them matters when you read the results. Domestic gas and electricity are measured, from meter readings published by the Department for Energy Security and Net Zero. Car and van emissions are derived, by combining vehicle registrations with mileages from MOT odometer readings. Everything else, including flights, food, goods, and services, is modelled, by matching a synthetic population built from census data to spending records in the Living Costs and Food Survey. The measured components are the most reliable and the modelled ones the least. The manual explains each in detail, and the analysis pipeline is published in full.

How far back does the data go?

It depends on the tool. Carbon footprints run from 2010. Public transport frequency in the Transport and Accessibility Explorer runs from 2004, with gaps in 2012 and 2013 where no timetable archive survives. Energy consumption in the Retrofit Explorer runs from 2015. The end year varies by dataset and is shown on each chart.

Which census does this use?

Mostly the 2021 Census for England and Wales and the 2022 Census for Scotland. All neighbourhood boundaries are the 2021 LSOAs and 2022 Data Zones, with earlier years mapped onto them. Some contextual datasets lag: the ONS area classification used for the "similar areas" comparison is still the 2011 version, which is a known weakness we intend to update.

How does this relate to the official DESNZ local and regional CO2 statistics?

They are different datasets built for different purposes, and they will not agree. The official statistics are territorial: they count emissions physically released within an area, including from industry and from traffic passing through. Carbon & Place is consumption-based: it counts the emissions caused by the people who live in an area, wherever in the world those emissions occurred, and excludes traffic that merely passes through. Neither is more correct; they answer different questions. Use the official statistics for territorial reporting obligations, and this tool for understanding what local residents' consumption implies.

Where do business and industrial emissions appear?

As part of residents' consumption, rather than as emissions at the site. The emissions of a car factory are attributed to the neighbourhoods where the cars are eventually bought, not to the neighbourhood containing the factory. This means a town with heavy industry does not carry those emissions in this tool, and it also means the tool cannot answer questions about industrial decarbonisation at a site. For that, use the territorial statistics.

Does this cover Scope 1, 2, and 3 emissions?

The scopes framework describes the emissions of organisations, so it does not map neatly onto a place-based footprint. The closest analogy is to imagine asking every resident of a neighbourhood to complete a detailed personal carbon footprint and then averaging the results: that includes what a company would call scope 1 (their gas boiler), scope 2 (their electricity), and much of scope 3 (everything they buy). It is not a substitute for an organisational inventory.

The largest parts of the footprint are consumption and flights, which are also the most heavily modelled. Is that a problem?

It is a real limitation, and worth stating plainly rather than defending. The categories with the best data, principally metered gas and electricity, are not the largest categories, and the largest categories rest on survey-based modelling. Three things follow. First, we present grades in wide bands rather than precise values, so that attention falls on large differences rather than on differences the data cannot support. Second, comparisons between places and trends over time are far more trustworthy than any single value for a single area in a single year. Third, each Methods tab states the specific weakness of that category, so you can judge how much weight to put on it. If you need a defensible number for one small area, the metered energy figures are the ones to use.

Are flight emissions based on residents' flying or on nearby airports?

On residents. Flights are allocated using the synthetic population and research on how flying varies with income and demographics, so living near an airport does not raise a neighbourhood's figure. Our estimates are higher than the Department for Transport's official figures for three reasons: the DfT counts only departing flights, whereas we count flights taken by British residents in either direction; the DfT counts only commercial aviation, whereas we include private flights; and the DfT counts carbon dioxide alone, whereas we use the UK government conversion factors including their radiative forcing uplift for emissions released at altitude, which roughly doubles the figure. Note that this is still conservative: Lee et al. (2021) and a CREDS briefing put aviation's full warming effect at around three times its CO2 alone, so a case can be made that our figures are too low rather than too high.

Could you add the Index of Multiple Deprivation, Mosaic, or Acorn?

Commercial segmentation products such as Mosaic and Acorn cannot be included in an open-source tool under their licences. Open datasets such as the Index of Multiple Deprivation could be added, but they are already well mapped elsewhere, so we have concentrated on producing data that does not otherwise exist at this scale. You can download our data and join it to any of these yourself.

Does the footprint account for carbon sequestration, for example by chalk grassland or woodland?

No. Carbon & Place measures emissions caused by residents and does not net off carbon stored in the land within a neighbourhood. For most urban neighbourhoods the quantities involved are small relative to the footprint, but that is not true everywhere: a rural area containing peatland or extensive woodland could store or release amounts that matter. Sequestration is also genuinely hard to estimate at this scale, and attributing land carbon to the residents of the LSOA that happens to contain it raises questions we have not resolved. Treat its absence as a gap in the tool rather than as a judgement that it does not matter.

What the tools can and cannot do

Can I benchmark my area against others?

Yes. Every neighbourhood report card compares the area with its local authority, with the national average for Great Britain, and with neighbourhoods of the same ONS area classification. The last of these is usually the most informative, because it compares like with like rather than setting a rural village against the national average. For larger areas, the Reports section provides ready-made summaries for local authorities, wards, parishes, and Westminster constituencies.

How are "similar areas" identified?

By the Office for National Statistics area classification, which groups neighbourhoods with similar social, economic, and demographic characteristics. Carbon & Place currently uses the classification derived from the 2011 Census; an updated version based on 2021 data exists and we intend to move to it.

Can I show only my own council area on the map?

Not at present. You can turn on the administrative boundary layers to see where your area begins and ends, and the Reports section produces a summary for a single authority. Filtering the map to one authority is a reasonable request that we have not yet implemented.

Can I combine local authorities, for example to get a county-level figure?

Not within the tool, but you can download the neighbourhood data and aggregate it yourself. One statistical warning: averaging the per-person values of a set of neighbourhoods is not the same as the per-person value for the combined population, because neighbourhoods differ in size. Weight by population to avoid that bias.

Does averaging to neighbourhoods really help target interventions?

It helps with a particular kind of question: what about this place, rather than these people, shapes the emissions here. An area with high car use and poor bus frequency is telling you something about the transport available, not about the willpower of residents, and that is a problem local government can act on. It is less useful for questions about individual behaviour, which neighbourhood averages cannot answer and should not be used to imply.

Can I use this to build a local carbon budget?

Not directly. The tools show historical and current footprints and include a reference line derived from the Sixth Carbon Budget, but they do not project forward or account for local targets, and the reference line divides the national budget equally per person rather than reflecting what would be fair or feasible in a given place. You can download the data and build your own budget from it. Downscaled local pathways, based on the Positive Low Energy Futures scenarios, are in development through the EDRC Futures theme.

Does the tool reflect the fact that many councils have set 2030 net zero targets?

Not yet. The tools describe what has happened rather than what is planned, and they contain no local targets. Adding scenario and target-tracking features is something we would like to do and is dependent on future funding.

Is the tool open source? Can I embed it in my own site?

The code is open source under the GNU Affero General Public Licence v3, so you may build your own version provided it is also open source. You can find it on GitHub. We do not permit embedding the hosted tools in third-party sites, partly because some of the underlying data licences do not allow it, but you are welcome to download the data and build your own visualisations, citing Carbon & Place as the source.

Rural neighbourhoods dominate the map because they are large. Can that be fixed?

It is a real problem with any map of areas that are equal in population but wildly unequal in size. Our partial answer is the dasymetric view, which is on by default: it colours only the built-up parts of each neighbourhood, so a large rural area no longer floods the map with a colour representing a few hundred people. A cartogram, which resizes areas by population, would address it more completely at the cost of geographical accuracy, and we have not had the resources to build one.
