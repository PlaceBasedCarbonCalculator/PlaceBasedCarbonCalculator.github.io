Historical domestic gas and electricity emissions

This chart shows the annual carbon emissions from domestic gas and electricity use in this postcode. Use the drop-down menu to select total, median, or mean emissions. Note that some areas are off the gas grid, so no gas emissions are reported; in these places heating may come from another fuel such as oil, wood, or coal. Emissions are calculated by Carbon & Place by multiplying metered energy consumption by the national emissions factor for each year. Because the electricity grid has been decarbonising, electricity emissions can fall even where consumption has stayed the same.

Emissions data at postcode level allows retrofit support to be targeted street by street rather than household by household. Area-based schemes tend to cost less per home than scattered upgrades, because survey, scaffolding, and installation costs are shared between neighbouring properties of similar construction, and because a visible local scheme is easier to trust than a national offer. This is the logic behind the local delivery routes within the Energy Company Obligation and the social housing decarbonisation programmes. It is worth noting the limit of this evidence: consumption tells you where energy is being used, not why, so it is a starting point for survey work rather than a substitute for it.

Postcode emissions are estimated by multiplying metered gas and electricity consumption by national emissions factors for each year. Electricity factors fall as the grid decarbonises, so identical consumption produces lower emissions in later years. Historical gas and electricity meters

This chart shows the total number of domestic gas and electricity meters in this postcode, with electricity meters also split into standard and Economy 7. In principle the total should equal the sum of the two categories; in practice reporting differences and privacy suppression produce small discrepancies, so both counts are shown rather than one being derived from the other.

Meter counts give a rough measure of how many homes a retrofit programme would have to reach in each postcode, and the balance between gas and electricity meters separates gas-heated streets from all-electric ones. The two need different approaches: gas-heated homes face a change of heating system, whereas all-electric homes, which include many flats and off-gas-grid properties, are more often a question of fabric, controls, and tariffs.

Meter counts come from the same DESNZ sub-national statistics. Meters do not map one-to-one to households: some homes have multiple meters, some meters serve communal areas, and vacant properties are still counted.

A change in the number of meters usually indicates house building or demolition, and a gap between the number of gas and electricity meters indicates homes that are not connected to the gas grid.

Typical energy bills

This chart shows the typical annual household energy bills for domestic buildings in this neighbourhood. Bills are calculated based on local consumption and typical pricing data. We have very limited data on other heating fuels such as oil or wood and so these bills are estimated.

Bills make the case for retrofit in terms households recognise, and they connect decarbonisation to the cost of living. Neighbourhoods with high bills are, other things being equal, where efficiency investment does most to reduce fuel poverty, and bill estimates can help target schemes such as the Warm Home Discount, the Energy Company Obligation, and local crisis support. One important caution: a low bill is not necessarily a good sign. Households that cannot afford to heat their homes properly show up here as low consumers, so this chart should be read alongside income, and the Energy bills vs Income map does exactly that.

Bills are estimated by applying average annual unit prices and standing charges (based on the Ofgem price cap and historical tariff data) to metered consumption. Actual bills vary with tariff, payment method, and supplier; the estimate assumes a standard tariff.

This tab summarises the domestic Energy Performance Certificates registered for this area. Since 2008, a home in Great Britain has needed an EPC whenever it is built, sold, or let. An assessor visits the property and records its construction and the efficiency of its heating, insulation, glazing, and controls, which makes the EPC register the only national dataset in which somebody has actually looked at the building. It is also, unavoidably, a flawed one. Modelled EPC energy use runs systematically higher than metered consumption in gas-heated homes (CREDS), and an audit of the register found at least one error in a majority of certificates (Hardy & Glew 2019).

EPCs are also not a representative sample of houses as they are only required under specific circumstances (e.g., when a house is sold). EPCs are likely to under-estimate the uptake of retrofit measures, as many people make home improvements just after buying a house. A certificate can therefore be out of date within months of being issued. For all these reasons EPC data should be treated with care. We include it because it is the best information publicly available about the fabric of British homes, and because the gap between what it can tell us and what a retrofit programme actually needs to know is itself worth making visible.

EPC ratings

The Energy Performance Certificate band is the headline result of an EPC assessment, rating a home from A (most efficient) to G (least efficient). It is worth knowing what the band measures: it scores modelled energy cost per square metre under standard occupancy, not measured energy use and not carbon. A well-insulated home on an expensive fuel can therefore score below a leakier home on a cheap one. Most existing homes in Britain sit in bands C and D, and the pathways set out by the Climate Change Committee assume large numbers of them improve over the coming decades.

EPC bands underpin much of housing policy: minimum standards for rented homes (currently E, with proposals to raise this to C; see the minimum energy efficiency standard), lender targets, and grant eligibility. Concentrations of D to G rated homes mark priority areas for area-based retrofit schemes.

The band reflects modelled running costs rather than carbon, so electrically heated homes can score worse than gas heated ones despite lower emissions, a known limitation of the current EPC methodology. Source: the domestic EPC register for England and Wales and the Scottish register, harmonised and matched to addresses by UPRN with our EPC processing code. The same three caveats apply to every EPC chart: coverage is partial, certificates age, and assessments contain errors. The manual explains what each means for interpretation.

Building type

The mix of built forms among the homes in this area that hold an Energy Performance Certificate: houses (detached, semi-detached, or terraced), flats and maisonettes, bungalows, and park homes. Built form is one of the strongest predictors of how much energy a home needs, because it determines how much external wall and roof each dwelling has.

Built form shapes what a retrofit programme can realistically offer. Flats usually require agreement from a freeholder or management company and often whole-building works, which is why they are frequently left out of grant schemes. Terraces share party walls and scaffolding, so they suit street-by-street delivery. Detached homes have the most external wall area per occupant to insulate, and also the most room for a heat pump and its pipework. Knowing the local mix is the difference between a programme that can be delivered and one that cannot.

Built form is recorded by the assessor at inspection. Tenure

A summary of tenure as recorded on the EPC. Note that tenure may have changed since the certificate was issued. EPCs are required whenever a property is let but only at the point of sale for owner-occupied homes, so rental properties tend to be over-represented in EPC data.

Tenure determines who is able to act, and through which policy lever. Owner-occupiers decide for themselves and respond mainly to advice, grants, and finance. Private landlords respond chiefly to regulation, in particular the minimum energy efficiency standard for rented homes. Social landlords can plan works across a whole portfolio and are the easiest route to retrofit at scale. The split-incentive problem, where a landlord pays for improvements from which the tenant gains, is the reason the private rented sector so often needs regulation rather than encouragement.

Tenure is self-reported at assessment and may lag changes of ownership or letting. Rented homes are over-represented in EPC data because letting triggers a certificate. Building Age

The estimated construction period of the homes in this area that hold an Energy Performance Certificate. Age is the single best guide to how a home is built and therefore to what retrofit it needs. For a count covering every dwelling rather than only certificated ones, see the Dwelling Stock tab.

Construction age is the best single predictor of how a home is built, and therefore of what it needs. Homes built before about 1919 usually have solid walls, which need internal or external insulation at several times the cost of filling a cavity. Homes built between the wars and the 1980s often have cavities that were never filled, which remain among the cheapest measures available. Homes built since the 1990s were built to progressively tighter Building Regulations and generally need little fabric work. An age profile is therefore the quickest way to size and cost a local retrofit plan.

Walls rating

About 35% of heat is lost through a house's walls, so insulating the walls is an excellent way to reduce energy use. Most homes built after the 1920s have cavity walls that are easy and cheap to insulate with blown glass fibre wool. However, care must be taken to install the insulation correctly to prevent damp crossing the cavity, particularly in places that get driving rain or have stone cavity walls. In these properties, expanding foam or polystyrene bead insulation can be more expensive but reduce the risk of damp and mould growth. Older homes with solid walls require either external or internal insulation. These options are more expensive than cavity wall insulation, and internal insulation requires redecoration of the rooms.

Fabric-first retrofit, insulating walls, roofs, floors, and windows before changing the heating system, cuts bills and emissions while improving comfort and health. Walls are the largest heat loss surface in most homes, so this data helps councils target area-based schemes (such as ECO flexible eligibility and social housing retrofit programmes) at the streets where poor wall performance is concentrated.

Wall energy ratings are the assessor's judgement of thermal performance from construction type and visible insulation. Walls description

A description of the construction of the external walls. The most common type is the brick or block cavity wall, which is cheap and easy to insulate with cavity wall insulation. Other wall types require more expensive internal or external insulation.

Wall construction dictates which measure is possible and what it costs. An unfilled cavity can usually be insulated in a day for a few hundred pounds. A solid wall needs insulation fixed to the inside or the outside, typically costing an order of magnitude more, and in the case of internal insulation requiring the room to be cleared and redecorated. The local split between cavity and solid walls therefore drives the cost of any neighbourhood programme more than any other single variable. Solid wall insulation also carries real risks of damp and mould if specified or installed badly, which is why government-funded schemes require a whole-house assessment under the PAS 2035 retrofit standard before work begins; CREDS reviews the technologies available for retrofitting the UK housing stock.

Wall construction is identified visually by the assessor and can be wrong where walls are rendered or clad. Roof rating

About a quarter of a home's heat is lost through the roof. For homes with pitched roofs, loft insulation is one of the cheapest and easiest efficiency measures, and uptake has been reasonably high thanks to its low cost and past government incentives. However, many homes have less than the recommended 300mm of insulation, and flat roofs remain difficult to insulate. Dwellings with another dwelling above, such as most flats, have no roof of their own and are recorded as "Dwelling Above"; only the top floor flat has a roof rating.

Topping up loft insulation is among the cheapest ways to save energy in an existing home, and the materials generally cost far less than the heat they retain over their life. Uptake is already high, so the value of this chart is in finding the homes where the cheap measure is still missing, which schemes such as the Energy Company Obligation exist to reach. Rooms in the roof and flat roofs are a different matter: both cost considerably more to treat and belong in a whole-house plan rather than a single-measure scheme.

'Above' means another dwelling is above (common for flats), so the dwelling has no roof of its own to insulate. Floor rating

Around 15% of a home's heat is lost through the floor. EPCs are especially poor at recording floor construction, so there is often missing data. Many homes built before the 1950s have suspended timber floors, which are relatively simple to insulate by fixing insulation to the underside from the crawl space or by lifting the floorboards. Homes built after the 1960s more often have solid concrete floors, which are difficult to insulate: the options are digging out the slab or insulating on top of it, which raises the floor level. This classification applies to ground floor properties, so flats are often recorded as "Dwelling Below".

Floors are usually the last fabric element worth addressing, because they account for a smaller share of heat loss than walls or roofs and are more disruptive to reach. Suspended timber floors can be insulated at reasonable cost where there is access from beneath; solid floors are rarely worth treating except during a major renovation, when the floor is coming up anyway. Floor data therefore refines an individual whole-house assessment rather than driving an area-based scheme.

'Below' means another dwelling is below, so there is no exposed floor to insulate. Floor Description

Ground floors of homes typically come in two types. Suspended floors are raised slightly above ground level and outside air can circulate in the void beneath the floor to prevent mould and damp. This was common for homes built before the 1940s. Conversely solid floors are built directly on the ground usually of concrete and are more common in post-1940s homes. Both floor types can result in heat loss if not insulated, but the way the floors are insulated is different. This chart shows the distribution of different floor types.

The distinction between suspended and solid floors decides what can be done. A suspended timber floor can be insulated from the void beneath, or by lifting the boards, at moderate cost and with a useful gain in comfort from reduced draughts. A solid floor can only be insulated by digging out the slab or by building up on top of it, which raises floor levels and affects doors and skirtings, so it is normally done only as part of wider works. This is refinement for a whole-house plan rather than the basis of an area-based scheme.

Floor construction is often inferred rather than inspected, so this is one of the less reliable EPC fields. Windows rating

Around 10% of heat is lost through windows and doors. Most houses now have double glazing, although much of it is of the less efficient early types. New double or triple glazing is an expensive option for energy saving. However, it is often installed as it is perceived to add value to the property.

Full window replacement is an expensive way to save energy, so policy attention focuses on the remaining single glazed homes, where the gains are largest and where conservation area rules may require secondary glazing or slim-profile units instead (Historic England advice). Draught proofing around existing windows and doors delivers much of the comfort benefit at a fraction of the cost.

Window ratings reflect glazing type and frame condition as judged by the assessor. Main heating system rating

This chart shows the efficiency rating of heating systems. An efficient heating system uses less energy than an inefficient system to provide the same amount of heating. Note that a very efficient heating system may not be low carbon. For example, a modern condensing gas boiler is over 90% efficient, yet it remains a high carbon heating source because it burns gas. The heating descriptions below give more detail.

Efficiency ratings show where the heating system itself is wasting energy, which is usually a matter of old non-condensing boilers, individual room heaters, or ageing electric storage heating. These are the homes where replacing the system cuts bills most. Note the limit of the measure, though: it rates efficiency rather than carbon, so a well-rated gas boiler still burns a fossil fuel and a poorly rated system on a clean fuel may already be low carbon.

The rating scores the system's efficiency, not its carbon: efficient gas boilers rate well despite burning fossil fuel. Main heating system description

Most homes in England have gas central heating, usually with a condensing boiler. Properly installed and maintained, these are over 90% efficient, but gas is a fossil fuel, so gas boilers need to be replaced with low-carbon heating such as heat pumps. Heat pumps are far more efficient than any boiler but cost more to install (very roughly £10,000, against £2,000 for a replacement gas boiler), which is why the Boiler Upgrade Scheme offers grants. Off the gas grid, homes heated by oil boilers, storage heaters, or coal fires face both high carbon emissions and high running costs, so heat pumps can be financially attractive sooner.

The mix of heating systems in an area determines the route its decarbonisation is likely to take. Areas dominated by gas boilers face conversion to heat pumps or connection to a heat network, which is the hardest and slowest of the routes. Areas heated by electric storage heaters are already off gas and can move to modern electric heating or heat pumps without touching the gas network. Buildings on communal systems are natural candidates for heat network zoning under the Heat and Buildings Strategy. The count of heat pumps is the most direct local measure of how far the transition has actually got, though it lags reality because it depends on when each home was last assessed.

Main fuel description

The main heating fuel recorded on local certificates. This is the single most consequential field for decarbonisation, because it determines both the emissions of each unit of heat and which policies and grants apply to the household.

Main heating fuel divides the stock into groups that different policies reach. Homes on mains gas are the largest group and the hardest to shift, awaiting heat pumps or heat networks. Homes on heating oil, bottled gas, or solid fuel have the highest emissions per unit of heat and the least protection from price rises, since these fuels are bought in bulk at unregulated prices rather than under a capped tariff; they are consequently a priority for Boiler Upgrade Scheme grants and for advice. Electrically heated homes need efficiency measures and help with tariffs rather than a change of fuel, and are over-represented among households in fuel poverty.

Heating Control Rating

Heating controls help save energy by turning off the heating when it is not needed. Common types of heating control include thermostats, timers, and Thermostatic Radiator Valves (TRVs). Upgrading heating controls can be a low-cost way to get a small reduction in energy use.

Heating controls, meaning programmers, room thermostats, thermostatic radiator valves, and zoning, are among the cheapest energy savings available, and unlike insulation they can be fitted in an afternoon without disruption. They also matter more than their cost suggests when converting to a heat pump, which works best running steadily at a lower flow temperature and therefore needs controls suited to that pattern rather than to a boiler. Concentrations of poorly controlled homes are straightforward targets for advice services and small grants.

Heating Control Description

Heating controls come in many combinations, and the most common are shown in this chart. A good heating system has a programmer, so you can decide when the heating runs; a thermostat, so the heating turns off once the house is warm; and Thermostatic Radiator Valves (TRVs) for room-by-room adjustment. More sophisticated systems add heating zones, enabling different settings in different parts of the home.

Lights Rating

Lighting is one of the easiest efficiency measures to adopt: modern LED bulbs are highly efficient and affordable. Solar Photovoltaics

Solar PV panels generate electricity from sunlight. While the cost of PV panels has dropped significantly in recent years the cost of installing them on the roof of a building can still be high. Homes with solar panels can use the electricity or sell it to the grid.

Rooftop solar reduces both bills and demand on the grid, and its uptake is strikingly uneven between neighbourhoods, reflecting roof orientation, tenure, and the capital available to households as much as the resource itself. Councils have several levers: bulk-buying schemes that reduce installation costs, requirements on new development through local plan policy, and installation across their own housing stock. Read the percentages with the coverage caveat in mind, since panels fitted after a home's last certificate do not appear. Solar Thermal

Solar Thermal panels provide hot water from sunlight. In the UK they usually pre-heat the water, with another technology such as an immersion heater bringing it up to full temperature. Even so, they can be a cost-effective way to reduce the energy needed for hot water, and they work on cloudy days as well as sunny ones.

Solar water heating is a mature technology that has become a niche one, largely overtaken by photovoltaic panels combined with an immersion diverter, which is cheaper per unit of energy and more flexible in what the electricity can be used for. Existing systems remain worth maintaining for the hot water they provide, but they are seldom the first recommendation for a new installation.

This section describes the dwelling stock recorded on the council tax registers, which is every home that exists rather than only those with an Energy Performance Certificate. It is the natural complement to the EPCs tab: less detail about each home, but no gaps in coverage.

Council tax bands

The number of dwellings in each council tax band, year by year. Because every home is banded, the total is a complete count of the dwelling stock, and its growth over time shows where new homes have been built. Bands are a rough proxy for property size and value, so the mix indicates the character of the neighbourhood: band A and B dominate in areas of small terraces and flats, band F and above in areas of large detached houses.

Band is used to target and means-test support: several energy efficiency and cost-of-living schemes have been restricted to lower bands as a rough proxy for household means. Bands also set council tax income, so the local mix shapes what an authority can fund. Note that the bands rest on 1991 values in England and Scotland, and 2003 in Wales, so they have drifted a long way from current prices.

England and Wales come from the Valuation Office Agency council tax stock statistics (CTSOP), published per LSOA. Scotland comes from the equivalent statistics.gov.scot dwellings-by-band series, published per 2011 Data Zone and apportioned here onto 2022 Data Zones in proportion to postcode counts, so Scottish figures for split or merged zones are estimates rather than counts. Band I exists only in Wales and is empty elsewhere.

Council tax band data is not available for this area.

Dwelling type

Every dwelling by built form: detached, semi-detached, terraced, flat or maisonette, bungalow, and the small residual categories. Unlike the EPC building-type chart, this counts all homes, not only those that have been sold, let or built since 2008.

Built form decides both what retrofit is physically possible and who has to agree to it. Flats generally need the freeholder's consent and often works to the whole block, an obstacle that has kept them out of most grant schemes despite housing a large share of lower-income households. Terraces suit street-by-street delivery, where survey and scaffolding costs are shared. Detached homes have the most external wall area to insulate and the most room for a heat pump and cylinder. Because this chart counts every dwelling, it gives a fairer picture of the local mix than the equivalent chart drawn from certificates.

From the Valuation Office Agency CTSOP3 table, per LSOA, England and Wales only, 2020 onwards. Type is recorded by the valuer, so conversions and unusual buildings can be classified inconsistently.

Bedrooms

Dwellings by number of bedrooms, from one to six or more. A useful proxy for dwelling size where floor area is not recorded, and for the kind of household an area can accommodate.

Dwelling size drives energy demand and the cost of retrofit more or less directly: a larger home has more wall and roof to insulate and more space to heat. The local size mix also bears on whether an area's housing matches the households who need to live in it, a question that runs through most local plan debates. Note that bedroom counts are a rough proxy for floor area, and that a room recorded as a bedroom by a valuer is not necessarily used as one.

Derived from the same VOA CTSOP3 table by summing bedroom counts across all dwelling types. England and Wales only, 2020 onwards. The count is of rooms the valuer recorded as bedrooms, which need not match how a household actually uses them.

Build period

When the homes in this area were built, from before 1900 to the present. Age is the single best predictor of how a home is built and therefore of what it needs: solid walls before roughly 1920, uninsulated cavities from then until the 1980s, and progressively better fabric since.

Age sorts neighbourhoods into retrofit packages. Areas of solid-wall housing need the expensive interventions that grant schemes have historically found hardest to deliver at scale, and where those homes are listed or in a conservation area, external changes are constrained as well as costly. Areas built between the wars and the 1980s have cavity walls, and where those cavities are still unfilled they represent the cheapest remaining measures in the stock. This chart covers every dwelling rather than only those with a certificate, so it is the more reliable basis for sizing a programme.

House prices set the financial context for retrofit. In high-value areas, households are more often able to borrow against the property or fund works from savings. In low-value areas the cost of a deep retrofit can exceed the value it adds to the home, which is the clearest economic argument for public subsidy: without it, the work will not happen however sound the case for it. Lenders have begun to reflect energy efficiency in mortgage products, though the evidence on how much of an efficiency premium is capitalised into prices remains mixed.

Transactions

The number of recorded property sales in this area each year. Volumes rise and fall with the housing market, and in areas with few homes a single year can be very small, so read the trend rather than any one year. Sales matter for retrofit because a sale is the point at which surveys, finance, and building work most often coincide.

Transactions matter for retrofit because a sale is the moment when a home is surveyed, finance is arranged, and building work is most likely to be contemplated. Several policy proposals, including minimum efficiency standards at the point of sale and green mortgage products, are designed around that moment. Transaction volumes therefore indicate how often each neighbourhood presents that opportunity: in areas where homes change hands rarely, a sale-triggered policy would take decades to reach the stock.
