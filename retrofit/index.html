Energy bills make retrofit tangible: neighbourhoods with high bills are where efficiency investment does most to reduce fuel poverty. Bill estimates can support the targeting of schemes such as the Warm Home Discount, ECO, and local crisis support.

This page summarises the Domestic Energy Performance Certificates from 2025. Since 2008, houses in the UK have had to get an Energy Performance Certificate (EPC) when they are sold or rented. An EPC requires a qualified assessor to visit the dwelling and take measurements of the building's construction and energy efficiency, which makes EPCs a valuable tool for understanding the energy use and carbon emissions from our homes. However, EPCs have significant problems. Research has shown that the EPC grades are not great at predicting real-world energy use and that as many as 60% of EPCs contain at least one error.

EPCs are also not a representative sample of houses as they are only required under specific circumstances (e.g., when a house is sold). EPCs are likely to under-estimate the uptake of retrofit measures, as many people make home improvements just after buying a house. Thus, the EPC may be out of date shortly after it is issued. For all these reasons, EPC data should be treated with care. We have chosen to include it here as it is the best available data and helps draw attention to the low quality of housing data in the UK.

EPC ratings

The EPC score is the headline result rating homes on a scale from A (most efficient) to G (least efficient). To meet the climate targets, we need all homes to be A or B graded as soon as possible.

EPC bands underpin much of housing policy: minimum standards for rented homes (currently E, with proposals to raise this to C; see the minimum energy efficiency standard), lender targets, and grant eligibility. Concentrations of D to G rated homes mark priority areas for area-based retrofit schemes.

Data comes from the domestic Energy Performance Certificate (EPC) register, cleaned and matched to neighbourhoods via UPRNs. Coverage is partial: only dwellings sold, let, or built since 2008 have a certificate (roughly two-thirds of homes), so owner-occupied and older homes are under-represented. Certificates reflect the most recent assessment, which may be years old, and assessor judgement introduces some inconsistency (Hardy & Glew 2019). See the manual for details. The band reflects modelled running costs rather than carbon, so electrically heated homes can score worse than gas heated ones despite lower emissions, a known limitation of the current EPC methodology.

Walls rating

About 35% of heat is lost through a house's walls, so insulating the walls is an excellent way to reduce energy use. Most homes built after the 1920s have cavity walls that are easy and cheap to insulate with blown glass fibre wool. However, care must be taken to install the insulation correctly to prevent damp crossing the cavity, particularly in places that get driving rain or have stone cavity walls. In these properties, expanding foam of polystyrene bead insulation can be more expensive but reduce the risk of damp and mould growth. Older homes with solid walls require either external or internal insulation. These options are more expensive than cavity wall insulation, and internal insulation requires redecoration of the rooms.

Fabric-first retrofit, insulating walls, roofs, floors, and windows before changing the heating system, cuts bills and emissions while improving comfort and health. Walls are the largest heat loss surface in most homes, so this data helps councils target area-based schemes (such as ECO flexible eligibility and social housing retrofit programmes) at the streets where poor wall performance is concentrated.

Wall construction dictates the measure: unfilled cavities can be insulated cheaply, while solid walls need internal or external insulation costing ten times more. The local cavity/solid split largely determines neighbourhood retrofit costs.

Roof rating

About a quarter of a home's heat is lost through the roof. For homes with pitched roofs, loft insulation is one of the cheapest and easiest efficiency measures, and uptake has been reasonably high thanks to its low cost and past government incentives. However, many homes have less than the recommended 300mm of insulation, and flat roofs remain difficult to insulate. Dwellings with another dwelling above, such as most flats, have no roof of their own and are recorded as "Dwelling Above"; only the top floor flat has a roof rating.

Loft insulation is one of the cheapest carbon measures available, with materials costing far less than the energy it saves, so remaining poorly rated roofs are quick wins for advice services and small grants. Room-in-roof conversions and flat roofs cost more to treat and suit whole-house retrofit plans. Roof condition data helps schemes such as ECO find the homes where a cheap measure is still missing.

Floor rating

Around 15% of a home's heat is lost through the floor. EPCs are especially poor at recording floor construction, so there is often missing data. Many homes built before the 1950s have suspended timber floors, which are relatively simple to insulate by fixing insulation to the underside from the crawl space or by lifting the floorboards. Homes built after the 1960s more often have solid concrete floors, which are difficult to insulate: the options are digging out the slab or insulating on top of it, which raises the floor level. This classification applies to ground floor properties, so flats are often recorded as "Dwelling Below".

Floor insulation is usually the last fabric measure worth doing: suspended timber floors can be insulated affordably where there is access beneath, while solid floors are rarely worth treating outside major renovation. In retrofit planning, floor data mostly refines whole-house assessments rather than driving area-based schemes.

Floor Description

Ground floors of homes typically come in two types. Suspended floors are raised slightly above ground level and outside air can circulate in the void beneath the floor to prevent mould and damp. This was common for homes built before the 1940s. Conversely solid floors are built directly on the ground usually of concrete and are more common in post-1940s homes. Both floor types can result in heat loss if not insulated, but the way the floors are insulated is different. This chart shows the distribution of different floor types.

Windows rating

Around 10% of heat is lost through windows and doors. Most houses now have double-glazing, although many have the less efficient early forms of double-glazing. New double or triple glazing is an expensive option for energy saving. However, it is often installed as it is perceived to add value to the property.

Full window replacement is an expensive way to save energy, so policy attention focuses on the remaining single glazed homes, where the gains are largest and where conservation area rules may require secondary glazing or slim-profile units instead (Historic England advice). Draught proofing around existing windows and doors delivers much of the comfort benefit at a fraction of the cost.

Main heating system description

Most homes in England have gas central heating, usually with a condensing boiler. Properly installed and maintained, these are over 90% efficient, but gas is a fossil fuel, so gas boilers need to be replaced with low carbon heating such as heat pumps. Heat pumps are far more efficient than any boiler but cost more to install (very roughly Â£10,000, against Â£2,000 for a replacement gas boiler), which is why the Boiler Upgrade Scheme offers grants. Off the gas grid, homes heated by oil boilers, storage heaters, or coal fires face both high carbon emissions and high running costs, so heat pumps can be financially attractive sooner.

The heating-system mix defines the decarbonisation route: boiler-dominated areas need heat pump conversion at scale, storage-heater areas can switch to modern electric heating more easily, and communal systems suit heat networks. Heat pump counts show how far the transition has actually progressed.

Heating Control Rating

Heating controls help save energy by turning off the heating when it is not needed. Common types of heating control include thermostats, timers, and Thermostatic Radiator Valves (TRVs). Upgrading heating controls can be a low-cost way to get a small reduction in energy use.

Better controls (thermostats, TRVs, programmers, smart zoning) are among the cheapest carbon savings available and can be delivered without disruption. Poor-control concentrations are quick wins for advice and small-grant schemes.

Lights Rating

Lighting is one of the easiest efficiency measures to adopt: modern LED bulbs are highly efficient and affordable. Solar Photovoltaics

Solar PV panels generate electricity from sunlight. While the cost of PV panels has dropped significantly in recent years the cost of installing them on the roof of a building can still be high. Homes with solar panels can use the electricity or sell it to the grid.

Rooftop solar cuts bills and grid demand, and uptake is highly uneven between neighbourhoods. Councils can enable group-buying schemes and require PV on new homes; the current very low percentages show the scale of the remaining opportunity.

Solar Thermal

Solar Thermal panels provide hot water from sunlight. Often in the UK they are pre-heating the water and some other technology (such as immersion heater) is needed to bring the water up to full temperature. However, they can be a cost effective way to reduce energy demand for hot water even on cloudy days.

Solar water heating is a mature but niche technology, largely superseded by PV plus immersion diverters or heat pumps. Existing systems are still worth maintaining for their hot-water savings.

The number of dwellings in each council tax band, year by year. Because every home is banded, the total is a complete count of the dwelling stock, and its growth over time shows where new homes have been built. Bands are a rough proxy for property size and value, so the mix indicates the character of the neighbourhood: band A and B dominate in areas of small terraces and flats, band F and above in areas of large detached houses.

Band is used to target and means-test support: several energy efficiency and cost-of-living schemes have been restricted to lower bands as a rough proxy for household means. Bands also set council tax income, so the local mix shapes what an authority can fund. Note that the bands rest on 1991 values in England and Scotland, and 2003 in Wales, so they have drifted a long way from current prices.

England and Wales come from the Valuation Office Agency council tax stock statistics (CTSOP), published per LSOA. Scotland comes from the equivalent statistics.gov.scot dwellings-by-band series, published per 2011 Data Zone and apportioned here onto 2022 Data Zones in proportion to postcode counts, so Scottish figures for split or merged zones are estimates rather than counts. Band I exists only in Wales and is empty elsewhere.

Council tax band data is not available for this area.

Dwelling type, bedroom and build-period breakdowns are published by the Valuation Office Agency for England and Wales only, so they are not available for this area. The council tax band chart above does cover Scotland.

Dwelling type

Every dwelling by built form: detached, semi-detached, terraced, flat or maisonette, bungalow, and the small residual categories. Unlike the EPC building-type chart, this counts all homes, not only those that have been sold, let or built since 2008.

Built form decides what retrofit is possible and who has to agree to it. Flats need the freeholder and often the whole block; terraces suit street-by-street schemes where scaffolding and survey costs are shared; detached homes have the most external wall to insulate and the most room for a heat pump.

From the Valuation Office Agency CTSOP3 table, per LSOA, England and Wales only, 2020 onwards. Type is recorded by the valuer, so conversions and unusual buildings can be classified inconsistently.

Bedrooms

Dwellings by number of bedrooms, from one to six or more. A useful proxy for dwelling size where floor area is not recorded, and for the kind of household an area can accommodate.

Dwelling size drives both energy demand and the cost of retrofit, and the local size mix is central to arguments about whether an area's housing matches the households that need to live in it.

Derived from the same VOA CTSOP3 table by summing bedroom counts across all dwelling types. England and Wales only, 2020 onwards. The count is of rooms the valuer recorded as bedrooms, which need not match how a household actually uses them.

Build period

When the homes in this area were built, from before 1900 to the present. Age is the single best predictor of how a home is built and therefore of what it needs: solid walls before roughly 1920, uninsulated cavities from then until the 1980s, and progressively better fabric since.

Age sorts neighbourhoods into retrofit packages. Solid-wall areas need the expensive interventions that grant schemes have historically struggled to deliver; cavity-wall areas built between the wars and the 1980s are the cheapest remaining wins. Pre-1900 stock is also more likely to be listed or in a conservation area, which constrains external changes.

From the Valuation Office Agency CTSOP4 table, per LSOA, England and Wales only, 2020 onwards. The single years from 2009 to 2021 are combined into one band. A substantial number of dwellings have an unknown build period, shown as its own category rather than distributed across the others.

Property Prices

A plot showing the distribution of property prices in the area over time. The maximum price line is hidden by default as it can overwhelm the chart. You can enable it by clicking on the legend. Note that this chart is only showing data about properties sold in a given year and so prices may fluctuate based on how many properties and which properties are sold.

House prices contextualise retrofit: high-value areas can often self-finance improvements, while low-value areas may face a viability gap where the cost of works exceeds the value uplift, justifying public subsidy. Energy efficiency is increasingly reflected in prices and mortgage lending.

Prices come from HM Land Registry Price Paid Data (England and Wales), matched to neighbourhoods via UPRN address geocoding. Only actual transactions are recorded, not valuations, so thin markets produce volatile averages, and Scotland is not covered by this dataset.

The upper and lower quartile lines in light blue show the interquartile range of property prices in each year. 50% of properties in each year fall within this range.

Transactions

A chart showing the number of property transactions in the area over time.

Transaction volumes indicate market activity and the natural trigger points at which retrofit is easiest to require or encourage: a sale is when surveys happen, finance is arranged, and building work is most likely, which is why proposals such as minimum standards at the point of sale and green mortgages focus on that moment.

Transaction counts come from HM Land Registry Price Paid Data (England and Wales). New-build and repossession sales have specific flags; some transaction types (e.g. right-to-buy, transfers not at market value) are excluded from the dataset.
